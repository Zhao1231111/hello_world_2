536382	1	
Summary: This work, LIV-Surfel, presents an incremental
LiDAR-inertial-visual 2D Gaussian Splatting back-end that uses
completed depth, monocular normal priors, candidate screening, and a
feed-forward network to predict surfel attributes at insertion time.
The network is trained by self-distillation from attributes obtained
after online optimization, and the paper reports improved novel-view
rendering metrics and early post-insertion convergence relative to
selected baselines.

Strengths: 
1. The paper identifies a key issue in incremental Gaussian mapping:
newly inserted primitives receive limited optimization updates before
impacting rendering. The proposed solution predicts insertion
attributes from the current rendering state and historical visual
context, effectively addressing this problem.
2. The surfel rotation design in Section III-C is sensible. Equations
(4)-(5) construct a surfel frame around a corrected normal, aligning
with the surface-oriented nature of 2DGS, rather than predicting an
unconstrained quaternion.
3. Table I shows results for R3LIVE, MCD, and a self-collected dataset,
with improvements over Gaussian-LIC2. Figure 3 visually confirms the
reduction of blur and trailing artifacts.

Weakness:
1. The evaluation does not confirm the system's real-time or online
feasibility, despite being framed as such. Sections I and III describe
an incremental mapping back-end for each keyframe, while Figure 1
outlines a complete online pipeline that includes various processing
steps. However, Section IV-A provides hardware specs but lacks crucial
per-module runtimes, keyframe processing times, rendering speeds, and
memory usage details. This is significant since the DA3 model and
multi-view feature extraction could add considerable computational
costs compared to simpler methods. The authors should include a
detailed timing breakdown, keyframe rates, map growth statistics,
memory peaks, and costs linked to the regressor and external networks.
2. The paper claims geometry-aware mapping but only evaluates
image-rendering metrics like PSNR, SSIM, and LPIPS. It lacks depth
error, normal error, and comparisons with LiDAR geometry, which are
essential to verify if the normal-guided rotation branch truly improves
surface geometry. The authors should include geometry metrics against
LiDAR and compare surfel normal consistency with and without the
normal-guided branch.
3. The ablation does not isolate the proposed regressor from other
system changes, limiting its ability to validate the general claim.
Table II compares "Baseline" versus "Ours" on hku_campus_00, while
Table III shows early rendering trajectories, indicating improvement
with the full learned-initialization configuration. However, it fails
to identify contributions from individual components (e.g. historical
context, cross-attention, and more)
and does not assess the impact of 2DGS or other elements on
performance. The authors should include component ablations across
multiple sequences, such as rule-based 2DGS initialization, direct
attribute regression without geometric priors, and others. 
 
536384	3	
The paper introduces LIV-Surfel, a feed-forward system for incremental
scene reconstruction relying on geometric priors and 2D Gaussian
Splatting surfels representation. The training is conducted in a
self-distillation fashion. Experiments are conducted on a bespoke,
R3LIVE, and MCD datasets, showing an improvement. 

S:

-	Introducing geometric priors from depth estimators is a
promising direction, orthogonal to only data-scaling
-	Using normals to constrain 2DGS surfaces sounds reasonable and
logical step
-	The simple design of geometric priors can have potential to be
easily adopted into other works
-	Straightforward combination of the multimodal setup

W:

-	The complexity of the system design.
As set out in "A System Overview" and Fig1, there is a multimodal setup
of IMU, Camera and LiDAR. The paper nevertheless argues that the sparse
LiDAR is insufficient to provide geometric guidance and thus use SPNet
to perform depth completion, and even then the paper argues that this
is still not a reliable source of information and finally use DA3 as
the actual depth estimator, while LiDAR serves only to establish the
scale. My question is then, is the LiDAR necessary at all? Since there
is IMU on board the scale for the DA3 can be established (IMU - yields
acceleration and angular velocity in physical units m/s² and rad/s).
Have you considered any ablation on that scenario? This will reduce the
method's hardware requirements.

-	Reliance on DA3
It might be that the superior performance of the presented method
relies on the powerful DA3 rather than on the novelty of the method
itself. It would be important to discern that. Have there been any
other depth estimators considered and compared? Is the performance
generally applicable to any depth estimators, or it largely varies
depending on their performance (especially stable normals)? Also, the
DA3 has an estimation of metric depth, please see also Sec 7.4
https://arxiv.org/pdf/2511.10647 and the used baselines -- this will
allow for reducing the system complexity to only cameras? I think the
paper would benefit from a discussion on the aspect of a) reliance on
DA3, b) comparison of the metric depth estimation vs metric
sensed/grounded from LiDAR/IMU c) the impact on the geometric prior if
swapped with 2-3 other estimators (accurate normal estimation seems to
be the key aspect)

-	Geometric priors’ choice
In the literature there are multiple geometric priors explored
throughout the years considering their uncertainty, using them
explicitly or implicitly (e.g., https://arxiv.org/pdf/2403.17822,
DN-Splatter at WACV,
https://proceedings.neurips.cc/paper_files/paper/2024/file/fc9f83d9925e
6885e8f1ae1e17b3c44b-Paper-Conference.pdf VCR-GauS at NeurIPS,
https://arxiv.org/pdf/2410.13862 DepthSplat at CVPR,
https://arxiv.org/pdf/2604.26238 EnerGS at ICML,
https://arxiv.org/pdf/2505.05591 QuickSplat at CVPR, just to name a few
directions). In the presented method design, there is little
justification for the selected set, which also seems quite
straightforward and using normals is quite popular and not new (as
above and in the RWs of those papers) – which is fair as long as there
is any evidence that supports this simplicity. Also, there is no
ablation provided later in the experiments. In the revision, I would
expect a detailed elaboration on the choice, supported with tables
and/or figures showing the benefits during training and/or inference
stages of that design, also preferably juxtaposed with more complex and
more abstract features, e.g., planarity (especially that the method
assumes 2D GS), curvature.
Also, the “validity mask” is considered as one of the geometric priors,
which is confusing for two reasons: a) the term “validity mask” is not
defined in the paper or at least I have not found it, please correct me
if I missed it b) how is the masked considered as part of the
“geometry”? Maybe it comes form my confusion regarding the definition.

- Computational overhead
The comparison concentrates on the accuracy results. Also, there is
visualisation for better and faster convergence, which is still rather
limited to one Fig 4 and Tab III, with only fixed 16 steps (but only
reported in Tab III and not present in Figure 4). Why this specific
number has been selected? Why the 16 step is missing in Fig4? And can
it also be that the priors bias to faster convergence which also leads
to being stuck in a (pre-mature) local minima? Also, there is no
comparison on the training time or inference time nor on parameters.
The paper would benefit from such analysis in comparison to selected
baselines.

-	Evaluation data
It seems that the evaluation is constrained to a very specific sensor
and system setup, mainly Livox. Also, the justification for the bespoke
dataset is not provided, with little details on the acquisition and
open sourcing after the submission acceptance. The paper would benefit
from stronger justification of the data used and testing on popular
benchmarks (comprising LiDAR) like Waymo or KITTI datasets, for
example.

-	Selected baselines
The paper argues that there are methods similar to their setup (II
RELATED WORK), however the compared methods seem to be quite limited
and only three baselines are selected for the comparison. I have not
found any justification for this set of baselines, and it seems
contradicting to the RWs part, where multiple RWs are listed. I would
expect adding a few related baselines from the authors’ RWs or
providing a justification for the limited set of baselines. Also, the
reported improvement is limited to only 1.28 dB PSNR, which might turn
out lower while extending the baseline comparison (which does not
necessarily invalidate the method contribution, but the pros and cons
should be clearly stated).

- Lack of Limitations Section
...which brings me to the lack of Limitations Section. The Limitations
should be explicitly stated in the paper to better position the paper
with its pros and cons witihin the large landscape of GS-related
methods. This will only improve the overall paper quality. 

Minor:
-	The yellow boxes in Fig 3 seem to be quite selective – the
differences should be observable throughout the sub-images 
-	“normal semantics” is used several times – it reads a bit
confusing, usually semantics pertains to classes / sem segments (more
abstract) rather than to only a geometrical feature

Paper Summary:
The paper proposes LIV-Surfel, a system designed to improve the
initialization of new Gaussians in incremental 3D Gaussian Splatting
(GS) mapping. The core problem addressed is that traditional rule-based
or random initializations often lead to local minima, "popping"
artifacts, and slow convergence during online optimization.

The authors introduce a feed-forward regression network that predicts
the initial attributes of 2D surfels (scale, rotation, opacity, and SH
coefficients) by leveraging multi-modal priors: dense depth completion
via SPNet and normal estimation via Depth Anything 3 (DA3). To bypass
the lack of ground-truth labels for Gaussian attributes, the authors
employ a self-distillation training strategy, using the refined state
of the map after optimization as the target. Experimental results on
RLIVE and MCD datasets indicate that LIV-Surfel achieves higher
rendering quality (PSNR/SSIM) and reaches convergence significantly
faster than baselines like MonoGS and Gaussian-LIC2.

Strengths:
- The focus on the "initialization bottleneck" in incremental GS is
highly relevant and well-motivated, as this is a known pain point for
real-time mapping stability.
- The use of cross-attention over a sliding window of keyframes allows
the model to capture temporal and multi-view consistency, which is
appropriate for the SLAM context.
- The self-distillation loop is an elegant way to train a regressor in
a domain where explicit labels are unavailable.
- The quantitative improvements and the convergence analysis provide
strong evidence that the method reduces the reliance on expensive
iterative optimization.
- The paper is well structured and presented.

Weaknesses:
- The most significant concern is the redundancy between SPNet and
Depth Anything 3 (DA3). The current pipeline runs two separate, dense
estimation paths: one for metric depth completion (SPNet) and one for
relative depth/normals (DA3).

Because DA3 already produces high-quality dense relative depth maps,
the authors and the authors already scale these outputs using the
sparse LiDAR points to obtain metric dense depth. This single path
would provide both the metric depth and the surface normals in one
shot. Running both SPNet and DA3 introduces GPU memory overhead and
latency without a clear justification for why a scaled-foundation-model
approach is insufficient.

- The authors emphasize that their method reduces the number of
optimization steps required (e.g. reaching thresholds in 4 steps vs
16). However, there is no analysis of the actual time cost of the
feed-forward inference pass. If the computational cost of running a
cross-attention network over multiple keyframes and two dense
estimation models (SPNet + DA3) exceeds the cost of those saved
iterations, the "efficiency" claim is purely theoretical and not
practically beneficial.

- The self-distillation loop assumes that the optimized map is a
reliable target. However, in incremental SLAM, drift is inherent. If
the optimizer overfits to a drifted state or produces artifacts, the
regressor will learn to predict these errors as truth. The paper does
not discuss how it prevents this confirmation bias loop or if any
filtering/regularization is applied to the distillation targets to
ensure only high-quality Gaussians are used for training.

- While Table 3 shows that the method converges faster, it is unclear
whether it reaches a better local optimum than the baseline after an
equal number of total steps (e.g., 32 or 64). If the regressor only
speeds up convergence to the same point, its value is purely
latency-based.

Questions for Authors
1- Why is SPNet required alongside DA3? Could the authors provide an
ablation study showing that scaling DA3's relative depth with LiDAR
performs worse than using SPNet?
2- What is the average inference time (ms) of the regressor per frame,
and how does this compare to the time saved by reducing optimization
steps? A comparison to the other method run times helps explain if the
quality improvements are "justified".
3- What is the parameter count and memory footprint of the regression
network? Is it viable for deployment on embedded systems common in
LiDAR-Inertial setups? and how does it compare to the existing methods?
